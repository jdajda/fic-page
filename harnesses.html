Canyoning Harnesses

Where the harness takes your weight changes how you move. A high anchor point and a low anchor point are not better and worse — they are answers to two different questions: where am I going, and how do I get back up?

Anchor points

High compared with low

Both configurations are standard. The decision is about the shape of the day, not about the quality of the equipment.

High

High anchor point

Pressure & balance zone
Middle abdomen
Intended for
Users who prioritize vertical progression in a downward direction while minimizing the risk of inversion.
Low

Low anchor point

Pressure & balance zone
Lower abdomen
Intended for
For experienced users who prioritize efficiency while ascending.

Why the difference matters

An anchor point placed high on the body keeps you upright when the rope is loaded. Descending, that is what you want: your weight hangs beneath the tie-in point, and the risk of being inverted — and therefore of being unable to reach your own descender — is reduced.

An anchor point placed lower moves the tie-in towards the hips. For a group used to climbing, that positioning is familiar and keeps the system efficient when the day involves a lot of ascending — for instance where a canyon is entered and left by repeated up-and-down routes.

🎯

The question to ask

Not "which harness is safer?" but "where is this group going?" A descent-heavy canyon favours the high anchor point; an ascent-heavy route favours the low one. Both are normal choices, and the FIC publishes both without ranking them.

The original plate

The full illustrated plate — showing both configurations racked in detail, with the pressure and balance zones marked on the body and the load paths drawn through the harness — is published by the FIC as a printable PDF.
