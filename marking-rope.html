Marking Rope

A rope that has been cut is no longer the rope you planned. Marking tells the next person — and the next group — exactly what they are holding, and how much of it is left.

FIC recommendation Middle mark Length marks
⚠️

Important

Always use a manufacturer-approved ink, because other inks may reduce rope strength. A marker pen left in a canyon is a tool; a marker pen that has destroyed your rope is a rescue.

Rope middle

Marking the middle of the rope

The middle mark is the point of the rope at which a rappel can be split safely — the place you can still get back from. It must be unmistakable, and it must be re-marked whenever the rope is shortened.

📍

Initial marking

A single band is marked at the rope middle, so that the two ends can be told apart and the point of a possible split is obvious to anyone using the rope.

✂️

After cutting

When a rope is cut — that is, after a reduction of rope length — the old middle is crossed out and a new middle is marked at the true centre of the shortened rope.

🧶

Option: strand differentiation

As an option, the middle can be marked by differentiating the rope strands rather than by adding a band — using a coloured strand next to a dark one, for instance.

💡

Why the option exists

Marking a band means touching the rope with ink. Where a rope is permanently rigged, marking by strand lets the middle stay visible without adding ink to a loaded section — which is exactly the section you least want to weaken.

Rope length

Reading a length at a glance

Bands grow as the distance from the end grows. The pattern is cumulative, so a rope can be measured by counting outwards from whichever end is nearest.

Interval Mark What you see
5 m Two narrow bands
10 m One band
50 m One thick band
100 m One extra-thick band

Worked example: a 185 m rope carries the 100 m band, then the 50 m band, then the 10 m band and the 5 m pair, on each side of the middle. Reading the bands outward from the middle gives the distance to that end without a tape measure.

🖼️
